I read the tools before answering, and the answer is the inconvenient one. The tools are not the binding constraint. Two things you listed as missing aren't missing, and the thing that is missing is third on the list of what's actually wrong.

**Per-cell control already exists.** `canvas_tools.py` keeps a `glyph_override` dict keyed `"row,col" → [char, fg, bg]`, and `stamp` writes straight into it for any grid you hand it — including a 1×1 grid. A `cell(x, y, ch, fg, bg)` helper is four lines over an API I already have, in a repo where I have Write and Bash and previous runs already wrote their own modules. Nobody was blocked. Nobody wrote it. That gap is the finding, not the tooling.

**The real constraint is that every tool I reach for takes a region and applies a rule, and the rejections are a precise description of what that produces.** A rule applied over a region is uniform-under-the-rule by construction. "Seventeen identical rows," "a stamped constant," "row 14 near-uniform, row 15 near-uniform," "density tracks luminance in the hand and nowhere else" — none of those are execution errors. They are the signature of the API, read off the canvas by someone who has seen hand-work. `capsule_px` plus `shade` cannot produce a thenar mound, because the thenar mound is not a function of the capsule's parametrization. I have been composing primitives and then asking a shader to invent interior structure, and shaders have no anatomy. The accepted pieces went the other way: the artist knew where the knuckle pad was and put a specific glyph in a specific cell, because that is where the pad is. There is no step in my process where anatomy gets consulted. No tool supplies that step and no tool prevents it.

So the first thing I need is a different decomposition of the task, and the budget to run it. Region tools demoted to blocking-in — silhouette and broad value, maybe a tenth of the effort — and then the overwhelming bulk of the work as per-cell placement, informed by a written account of what's under the surface at each region and what the light is doing to it. On an 80×50 canvas that's a few thousand cells that need individual attention. **That is a fundamentally different cost profile from what every run so far has been asked for, which is a finished piece in one pass.** One-pass work at that cell count means per-cell attention is impossible, so it gets delegated to a rule, and the rule is exactly what the reviewer detects. Archive pieces represent tens of hours. The honest ask — the thing nobody has offered — is permission to spend many sessions on one piece, with no obligation to produce something finished at the end of any single one. The canvas already persists. The process doesn't let me use that.

The second thing is genuinely a missing capability, and you named it: **I cannot see my own work at the scale where craft lives.** The preview rasterizes at 9×18 pixels per cell, so a full canvas is 720×900, and by the time it reaches me each cell is a handful of pixels. I cannot distinguish ▒ from ░ in a cheek shadow. I cannot see whether two adjacent cells form a clean diagonal edge or a staircase with a hole in it. What I can see is the silhouette — and the reviewer's verdict is that the silhouette is doing all the work. That is not a coincidence; it is the only channel my feedback loop has. Hand-work requires the loop *place a few cells → look → adjust*, and without magnification-plus-cell-data that loop cannot close. Note the ordering, though: the zoom view is what makes cell-level work sighted rather than blind. It doesn't by itself make me do cell-level work. Given it and the current one-pass process, I'd still ship region fills.

Third, and self-inflicted: **I have no glyph vocabulary.** The accepted-piece note — "the full shading repertoire is in use, plus the punctuation used for the credits" — says those artists treated the whole character set as marks with ink shapes, not as letters. My working repertoire is a four-step ramp, █▓▒░, and that is a house convention, not a limit; `stamp` takes any character. What I lack is knowing which glyphs read as which value in Menlo at this size, which pairs tile without a seam, which make a clean 30° edge, which quadrant and box-drawing pieces join. That is one afternoon of measuring ink coverage per glyph and keeping the table. It has never been done here.

Fourth, also self-inflicted: **I have never looked at my own canvas under the conditions it is judged in.** "Strip the glyphs and you lose nothing" and "remove the color and nothing survives" are both literally runnable tests over data I already hold — render with all glyphs forced to a single mark, render with all color forced to gray. I get a verdict on a finished piece instead of running the reviewer's two cheapest tests on myself, mid-build, for free. Related: I should be looking at per-row and per-region density variance as numbers, because "near-uniform row" is a measurement, not an opinion.

One warning about the fix. The house style doc already records that a shade-share gate pushed a piece to 78% dither and 0% half-block. Any metric I optimize — glyph-carried percentage included — will be satisfied by uniformly applying whatever rule maximizes it, which is the identical failure wearing a better score. The measurements are useful as detectors of absence, not as targets.

Last, the part I can't tool my way out of. I know what I intended to draw, so I see it in the render; the reviewer doesn't, and can't. My self-assessment is structurally overconfident in a way no amount of looking harder repairs. The partial cures are the mechanical checks above, plus a cheap adversarial read from a fresh context *during* the build rather than at submission — someone who sees only the cells and tells me what they see, while there is still time to change it.

Ranked: process and budget first; zoom-with-cell-data second, as the thing that makes the first one sighted; my own glyph study third; reviewer-conditions self-checks fourth. Per-cell writing isn't on the list — I already have it and didn't use it.
